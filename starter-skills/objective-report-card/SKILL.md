# Objective Report Card

Produce a defensible judgment about whether the reviewed work achieves the user's objective, and identify what would materially improve it. Adapt the criteria to the domain; do not impose one universal rubric.

## Establish the standard

- Identify the objective, intended audience or decision-maker, stage, and object being graded. Distinguish the quality of a presented case from the underlying system or business it describes.
- Use the user's rubric when supplied. Otherwise derive a small set of consequential criteria from the objective and define success before scoring. State material assumptions; ask only when ambiguity would materially change the judgment.
- Define an A as sufficient, convincing achievement of this objective at this stage, not perfection or an enterprise-scale wishlist. Identify non-negotiable decision gates separately from supporting criteria.
- Use relevant domain expertise and current authoritative sources when needed. Do not force web research or a broad audit when the supplied material supports the requested assessment. Domain skills may inform criteria without taking over the task or expanding its scope.

## Ground the judgment

- Inspect the actual artifact or system within the authorized scope. State the version, date, environment, or sample when relevant. Existing results are not newly executed tests; an artifact's claims are not independent verification.
- Distinguish observed evidence, reported claims, inference, and unknowns where the distinction affects a grade. Cite the source supporting material findings; name inspection limits.
- Separate missing evidence from demonstrated failure. Use **Unverified** when underlying performance cannot be assessed. If the objective itself requires presenting proof, missing proof can lower that criterion while underlying performance remains unknown.
- Tie each grade to an explicit gap against the standard. Avoid false numeric precision and unsupported letter modifiers.

Default anchors, unless the user supplies another scale:

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Meets the objective with convincing evidence; remaining changes are minor. |
| B | Substantially meets it, with a material gap or qualification. |
| C | Partially meets it; substantial gaps limit the intended outcome. |
| D | Major deficiencies prevent the intended outcome. |
| F | Evidence demonstrates fundamental failure against the criterion. |
| Unverified | Available evidence cannot support a performance judgment. |

## Make the result actionable

Lead with the overall judgment and its practical implication. Prefer this compact table, adapting it to the user's requested format:

**Criterion | Grade | Evidence and gap | What earns an A**

- Make “What earns an A” observable: a specific change, decision, or proof that closes the gap. Distinguish improving the artifact from work needed to establish the underlying capability. If already A, say so without inventing extra work.
- Explain how consequential gaps drive the overall grade. Do not silently average grades or let strong presentation offset a failed decision gate. If the user requests weighted scoring, show the weights and retain a separate readiness judgment.
- End with the highest-impact next steps, enough to reach the next grade and no padding, ordered by their effect on the objective and relevant dependencies. Include a way to verify completion. Avoid inventing owners, deadlines, budgets, or commitments.
- Scale detail to the request. A short report card need not become a research report, implementation plan, or workshop. Assessment alone does not authorize fixes or external actions; continue implementation when the user has separately authorized it.

## Reassess consistently

Reuse the prior objective, criteria, anchors, and weights when available. Compare new evidence with the prior baseline, show meaningful grade changes, and identify unresolved gates. Do not raise grades merely because recommendations were implemented. Verify their effect at the level the objective requires.

If the objective or evidence standard changes, explain the change and avoid presenting the resulting grades as a directly comparable improvement. If the previous rubric is unavailable, label the new assessment as a fresh baseline.
